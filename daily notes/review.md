# Paper Summary*
This paper introduces MedVideoCap-55K, a large-scale, caption-rich medical video dataset comprising 55,000 curated medical video clips sourced from YouTube through a multi-stage filtering process. Additionally, the paper introduces MedGen, a domain-adaptive video generator obtained by fine-tuning the HunyuanVideo model on this dataset using LoRA. The authors report that MedGen performs exceptionally well on the VBench and VideoScore benchmarks, outperforming 15 open-source models and 4 proprietary models. They also conducted a physician-based evaluation, which demonstrated that MedGen achieves improvements in both medical accuracy and text alignment. Furthermore, they demonstrate that MedGen can be transferred to another foundation model (Mochi-1-preview) and achieves some improvements in downstream classification through synthetic data augmentation.


# Paper Strengths*
- This issue is of significant importance in the field of medical imaging. Existing T2V models do indeed produce anatomical errors and misidentifications of medical devices when trained on medical prompts.
- The experiments are thorough and well-designed. The authors compared 15 open-source baselines and 4 commercial baselines using VBench and VideoScore. In addition to traditional metrics, they incorporated evaluations by human physicians, an approach that is well-founded and highly valuable to the field.


# Major Weaknesses*
Although this is a medical imaging paper in the datasets/benchmark track, my view is that the authors attempted to focus on both model methods and data, resulting in a somewhat unclear introduction to the dataset and leaving room for optimization in the dataset pipeline.

- Medical accuracy of captions is not fully validated. The paper's key selling point is being "caption-rich" (Abstract, L013, L102), but all captions are auto-generated by GPT-4o, which is known to hallucinate in medical contexts. The Ethics Statement (L380-381) only mentions verification for "inappropriate/identifiable material"—that's compliance, not medical correctness. No error rate from expert review, no comparison against reference annotations, no discussion of caption noise impact on training. For a medical dataset whose main contribution is caption quality, this is a critical omission.
- The pipeline in Section 3 is essentially a direct reuse of general-purpose video dataset workflows. All the modules (keyword + snowflake-arctic-embed, CLIP frame classifier, CLIP similarity, OpenCV, EasyOCR, LAION, Dover, GPT-4o captioning) are off-the-shelf tools also used in Panda-70M and OpenVid-1M. The only medical-specific parts are the keyword dictionary and the frame classifier's medical labels, and neither is documented in detail (size, construction, training data, and accuracy are all unreported). No module specifically addresses the unique challenges of medical video, such as anatomical continuity, surgical stage boundaries, clinical scene compliance, or identifiable patient information. This significantly weakens the methodological contribution expected from a Datasets track submission.
- The supplementary material does not provide enough analysis of the dataset itself. Given the Datasets track choice, the supplementary would ideally focus on dataset documentation, but Section A is only 3 lines (L517-519) and just refers back to Fig. 4, which does not quite match the promise in L203 that "more data analysis is provided in the Appendix." Meanwhile, Sections B.1 and B.2 (training details and baseline links) take up most of the space, giving the impression that the focus is on MedGen rather than the dataset. It would be helpful to include a standard Datasheet (Gebru et al., 2021), per-subcategory statistics, an analysis of source diversity such as the number of unique YouTube channels, and a cross-tabulation by medical discipline, since the current categories are based on video format rather than medical content.
- The "Evaluation & Benchmark" contribution feels relatively weak on its own. Med-VBench is essentially VBench with the aesthetic score removed, and VideoScore is reused without modification. The human physician evaluation is a nice highlight, but the scoring criteria for Medical Accuracy and Text Alignment are not defined anywhere in the paper or the Supplementary, so it is hard to tell what standard the three physicians actually used for judging medical accuracy, which is the core dimension the paper emphasizes.

# Minor weakness
- Section 3.3 states, “As shown in Figure ??,” which may be due to the author not resolving a LaTeX compilation error.
- Supplement B.3 provides a set of Kappa values (0.82 / 0.79 / 0.77), and Table 10 in B.4 provides another set (0.83 / 0.81 / 0.78), but it does not specify which evaluation task each set corresponds to. Readers must infer this on their own. I recommend clearly labeling them.
- In Table 3, MedGen’s total score is 2.57, Sora’s is 2.96, and Kling’s is 2.80—a significant gap. The phrase “rivals commercial systems” is somewhat of an exaggeration; wording such as “narrows the gap” would better reflect the data.

# Preliminary Recommendation*
3 boaderline reject

# Justification Of Preliminary Recommendation*
This paper is submitted to the Datasets/Benchmarks track, with MedVideoCap-55K as its main contribution. The motivation is valid—large-scale, caption-rich medical video datasets are indeed lacking—and the authors put in substantial effort, producing a 55K-scale resource whose utility is supported by the downstream augmentation and transfer experiments. However, judged against the core criteria of this track, the submission has several notable shortcomings: captions are generated entirely by GPT-4o without any expert validation of medical accuracy, which is a serious concern for a medical dataset; the pipeline is largely a direct adaptation of general-purpose video dataset workflows and lacks domain-specific design; the supplementary material offers almost no documentation of the dataset itself—no datasheet, category breakdown, or source diversity analysis; and the release format, licensing, YouTube compliance, and patient privacy are not clearly addressed. In addition, the claimed "evaluation protocol" contribution is essentially a combination of existing tools, and the scoring rubric for Medical Accuracy is missing. Overall, the research question is valuable, but the paper is not sufficiently rigorous in the dimensions most central to the Datasets track—data quality validation, documentation, and compliance—hence a score of 3. I would consider raising my score if the rebuttal provides expert validation of caption accuracy, clarifies the release format and license, and supplies more complete dataset documentation.


# Suggestions For Rebuttal*
- Please provide an expert (physician) validation of caption medical accuracy on a random sample (e.g., 200–500 clips), and report the error rate and typical error types. This is the single biggest concern given that captions are entirely GPT-4o generated.
- Please clarify the release plan: will you release the video files themselves, or only YouTube URLs + timestamps? Under what license? And how do you ensure compliance with YouTube's ToS given the source of the data?
- Please provide the scoring rubric for Medical Accuracy and Text Alignment used in the human evaluation. Supp Table 9 only covers distortion, which leaves the core "Medical Accuracy" claim without a defined standard.
- Please expand Supplementary Section A with proper dataset documentation—at minimum a datasheet, per-category statistics, and the number of distinct source channels. The current 3-line section does not match the "more data analysis in the Appendix" promise in L203.
